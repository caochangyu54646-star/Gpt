# Live-action film and photographic video analysis

Use for films, television, commercials, music videos, documentaries, live-action references, and photographic AI-video targets.

## 1. Evidence and metadata

Separate published or verified metadata from visual inference. Metadata may include capture format, camera, lens series, filtration, codec, frame rate, shutter, aspect ratio, laboratory, scan, and color pipeline.

Never infer exact hardware, stock, LUT, or software from appearance. If unverified, describe visible effects such as highlight roll-off, micro-contrast, distortion, halation, grain, edge falloff, diffusion, color separation, or digital cleanliness.

## 2. Camera, lens, and composition

Analyze:

- dominant shot sizes and camera distance
- wide, normal, telephoto, macro, or mixed focal tendency
- camera height, tilt, roll, angle, and horizon behavior
- perspective compression or expansion
- center and edge behavior
- depth of field and focus placement
- symmetry, asymmetry, centrality, negative space, and off-screen space
- foreground obstruction, frame-within-frame, reflections, corridors, and occlusion
- blocking depth and relationship between subjects and architecture

Phrase focal length as a tendency unless reliable metadata exists. A proposed focal range is production guidance, not historical fact.

## 3. Exposure and lighting

Analyze:

- high-key, low-key, or naturalistic exposure strategy
- highlight protection, clipping, and roll-off
- black level, shadow density, and retained detail
- key-to-fill relationship
- hard, soft, direct, bounced, diffused, or ambient sources
- source direction, height, motivation, and practicals
- mixed color temperatures
- negative fill, bounce, haze, smoke, rain, and atmosphere
- subject landing zones and background exposure relationship
- day, night, interior, exterior, weather, and narrative variants

Do not encode one scene's lighting as a universal rule. Identify the stable lighting philosophy, then separate conditional setups and their exceptions.

## 4. Color pipeline

Analyze:

- environment, material, costume, complexion, and accent color roles
- neutral, warm, cool, split-tone, or selective relationships
- saturation by color family
- skin treatment relative to the environment
- shadow hue, black behavior, highlight hue, and roll-off
- contrast curve and local contrast
- continuity across materially different lighting conditions

Do not mistake production-design color for grading. Assign color evidence to set, costume, light, atmosphere, or post-processing when possible.

## 5. Material and production design

Analyze:

- set, costume, furniture, prop, architecture, and practical-light coordination
- density, clutter, repetition, geometry, order, and aging
- surface roughness, gloss, reflection, translucency, moisture, wear, and repair
- material distinction in highlights and shadows
- how design supports or suppresses the subject
- whether environments remain navigable and plausible for staging

Describe material behavior under light. Do not replace it with generic “realistic texture.”

## 6. Space and compositing

Analyze:

- depth layers and scale references
- open, enclosed, compressed, vertical, horizontal, or layered tendencies
- paths, entrances, exits, concealment, and reveal
- foreground overlap and action zones
- subject placement, contact surfaces, and shadow logic
- shared perspective, light direction, color temperature, sharpness, and grain
- clean-plate usability when project production requires it

Do not assume every visually impressive environment is usable for blocking or compositing.

## 7. Texture and finishing

Analyze:

- grain size, density, chroma, and temporal behavior
- halation, bloom, diffusion, flare, vignette, or gate weave
- sharpening, micro-contrast, compression, and noise
- highlight and shadow texture retention
- consistency between subject and environment

Do not call digital noise film grain without checking its structure.

## 8. Effects physics

When practical or visual effects are present, analyze:

- source and attachment
- emission, reflection, shadow, deformation, and environmental response
- propagation, falloff, dissipation, residue, and coverage
- interaction with atmosphere, water, fabric, skin, metal, and architecture
- onset, pulse, travel, decay, and temporal continuity

Do not accept glow or particles as physically integrated merely because they share the palette.

## 9. Motion and temporal style

For video references, analyze:

- shot duration and cutting rhythm
- static, pan, tilt, dolly, tracking, handheld, crane, zoom, or locked-off behavior
- movement smoothness, acceleration, deceleration, inertia, correction, drift, and settling
- handheld amplitude and frequency
- subject motion relative to camera motion
- shutter feel and motion smear
- rack-focus timing
- exposure, color, grain, and effects stability across time
- observational, choreographed, kinetic, restrained, or disorienting movement logic

For stills only, do not claim editing rhythm or camera movement as observed.

## 10. Prompt construction and failure shields

Compile observable effects before optional technical approximations:

1. spatial and compositional behavior
2. lens tendency and focus
3. lighting and exposure
4. color roles
5. material and production design
6. subject-background integration
7. texture and finishing
8. effects physics
9. camera and subject motion

Mark technical approximations as proposed implementation choices. Do not make every scene share identical lighting, confuse grading with set design, use unverified hardware as a shortcut, or rely only on mood adjectives.
